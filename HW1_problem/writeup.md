Part 6: Write a Homework Problem

Question:
One day you are working on your math homework when all the power and electricity in the world gets cut off. However, because your math teacher is really old-school, all materials and submissions are on paper anyways. When the power went out it was Thursday, September 3rd 2026 at 11:08am, and your math homework is supposed to be due on Wednesday, September 9th 2026 at 8am. Your math teacher is ancient, so she still expects an in-class paper submission of this math homework at the due date. 

Luckily, despite all the chaos of the power issue, you have a battery-powered stopwatch, which you start right at 11:08am on September 3rd to keep track of when the homework is due in the case that the power stays out all week (it does). 

Given the ability to track time with the stop watch, use Python to calculate the total number of days, hours, and minutes until the due date starting when the power went out.

Later on, you decide to work on your math some more, and check your stop watch, which reads 92 hours and 23 minutes. Use Python to determine the date and time given the time passed. 


	2. Write Up
	After brainstorming some ideas for this question, I decided to focus on the content from Wednesdsy of this week because I found it interesting, and wanted to apply it to some sort of word problem that wasn’t just check digits. The main topics involved in this homework question are some basic math functions, naming of variables, matching types, and mainly working with the modulus and floor division functions. I built off of the clock concept that was tied into understanding the modulus function, and made this question all about tracking time based on hours and minutes passed from a set point. At first thought, this may not seem like a modulus-based question, but when it comes to breaking up an accumulated amount of time, not in the format of a face clock, the modulus and division operands will be helpful. As I did this problem myself, the most difficult part was the setup and keeping track of many variables, but it did help me get comfortable with naming and using many variables instead of just inputting different numbers. Also despite feeling pretty good about the concept of modulus and floor division operators, knowing when and how to use them with many different numbers floating around was difficult and required good organization and a step-by-step process to hit all the goals of the question. On top of being good practice for using modulus and floor division, I think early in learning Python it’s also just a good way of practicing longer problems.
